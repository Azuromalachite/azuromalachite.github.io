I am Thanakrit (Austin) Wongsatit. Originally from Thailand, I am a PhD student in Engineering Biology at KAIST in Daejeon, South Korea.

My research sits at the intersection of microbial ecology, evolution, and biological engineering. I am interested in how biological systems function, interact, and evolve across scales, from proteins to microbial communities, and in developing experimental and computational tools to study and engineer them.

My current work spans microbiome engineering, protein mining, anaerobic cultivation, laboratory automation, and long-read sequencing. More broadly, I am interested in how biological engineering can draw more deeply on the ecological and evolutionary properties of living systems. Rather than treating context, variation, and evolution only as sources of noise, I am interested in what they can teach us about how biological systems should be studied and designed.

> “the first generation of scientists with the tools to address the dimensions of biodiversity on Earth might also be the last with the opportunity to do so.”
>
> — James P. Collins, 2009

That idea resonates strongly with how I think about biology. The molecular tools now available to us make it possible to study biological diversity at extraordinary resolution, but understanding that diversity also requires paying attention to organisms, their environments, and their evolutionary histories.

## Background

Before beginning my PhD, I worked as a research assistant at VISTEC with Chayasith Uttamapinant. Across my internship and subsequent research there, I worked on protein characterization for CRISPR diagnostics, metagenomic mining of plastic-degrading enzymes, and microscopy image processing for phase-separation systems.

My interest in molecular biology became much more tangible during high school at Kamnoetvidya Science Academy (KVIS). A BioXCepTion molecular docking workshop organized by Bundit Boonyarit introduced me to proteins as nanoscale molecular entities that could be visualized and explored on a computer. I later joined the team that grew out of the workshop and helped teach a subsequent BioXCepTion workshop at KVIS.

Another formative experience was Jason Chin’s 2018 seminar at VISTEC on genetic code expansion. I was fascinated by the idea that something as evolutionarily conserved as the genetic code could nevertheless be experimentally expanded through the addition of new amino acids to the translation system and the use of quadruplet codons. Questions after the seminar led me to meet Chayasith Uttamapinant, whom I later approached about an internship after finishing high school.

In early 2020, I took a semester-long leave of absence from KAIST and joined an effort to establish a CRISPR diagnostics platform in Thailand. That work also led to my first contribution to a scientific publication.

Around the same period, an attempt to revive an iGEM initiative at KVIS evolved into a different project: **Get Rich with SynBio (รวยด้วย SynBio)**, a Thai podcast about synthetic biology. I spearheaded the project, coordinated meetings, and designed its visual materials while working with Pakpoom Subsoontorn, Cholpisit Kiattesewee, and Nattawat Leelahakorn.

## Natural history

Alongside experimental and computational biology, I maintain a strong interest in natural history.

I work particularly on Southeast Asian blue-banded bees of the genus *Amegilla*. Through observations, photography, literature study, specimen comparisons, and ongoing conversations with wild-bee researchers in Thailand, including Natapot Warrit and Chawatat Thanoosing at Chulalongkorn University, I contribute to efforts to resolve difficult species identifications and taxonomic questions in the region.

The difficulty is not new. Writing about the *Anthophora zonata* group in 1929, Cedric Dover remarked that its taxonomy was already “in a very confused state,” and argued that:

> “any attempt to provide more than a working classification seems to me to be futile without a very large series of specimens from all parts of their geographical range, and without extensive ecological and experimental observations.”
>
> — Cedric Dover, 1929, *Wasps and bees in the Raffles Museum, Singapore*

Nearly a century later, that observation still captures much of what makes Southeast Asian *Amegilla* so interesting to me. Resolving these bees requires not only comparing morphology, but also assembling observations across geography, revisiting historical literature and specimens, and eventually connecting taxonomic variation with ecology.

This work has become an important second strand of my scientific life. Taxonomy operates at a very different scale from molecular biology, but I find many of the underlying habits surprisingly similar: paying attention to variation, distinguishing meaningful signals from noise, reconstructing relationships, and understanding organisms in their ecological and historical context.

I also enjoy nature photography, particularly as a way of paying closer attention to organisms and their environments.

## Design

Visual design has accompanied my scientific work for many years. I designed graphics and posters for **Get Rich with SynBio (รวยด้วย SynBio)** in 2021–2022 and continue to be interested in scientific communication, information design, and visual systems.

I am especially drawn to design as a way of making complex systems legible without removing their complexity. Scientific questions, visual communication, and biological observation therefore recur throughout much of what I do.
