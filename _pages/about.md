I am Thanakrit (Austin) Wongsatit, originally from Thailand and currently a PhD student at the Graduate School of Engineering Biology at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) in Daejeon, South Korea, working under the supervision of Professor Sung Sun Yim. I hold a B.S. in Bio and Brain Engineering, with a minor in Biological Science, also from KAIST.

My scientific journey began during high school at Kamnoetvidya Science Academy (KVIS) in Wang Chan Valley, Rayong, next to the Vidyasirimedhi Institute of Science and Technology (VISTEC). A molecular docking workshop there first introduced me to proteins as nanoscale molecular entities that could be visualized and explored on a computer. Around the same time, Jason Chin’s 2018 seminar at VISTEC on genetic code expansion fascinated me with the possibility of experimentally extending something as evolutionarily conserved as the genetic code.

After graduating from KVIS, I interned at VISTEC with Chayasith Uttamapinant, where I worked on protein characterization for CRISPR diagnostics and metagenomic mining for novel plastic-degrading enzymes. In early 2020, I also took a semester-long leave of absence from KAIST to help establish a CRISPR diagnostics platform in Thailand, which led to my first contribution to a scientific publication. I later returned to VISTEC as a research assistant in 2024, working on microscopy image processing and analysis for protein phase-separation systems.

Today, my research focuses on developing tools and methods for microbiome engineering, using approaches that span protein mining, anaerobic cultivation, long-read sequencing, laboratory automation, and computational biology. More broadly, I am interested in how biological systems function, interact, and evolve across scales, from proteins to microbial communities, and in how biological engineering can draw more deeply on the ecological and evolutionary properties of living systems.

> We are the first generation of scientists with the tools to address the dimensions of biodiversity on Earth…and ironically we may be the last generation with the opportunity to discover and understand Earth’s biodiversity before it is irrevocably changed or lost.
>
> — James Collins, February 13, 2009

National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. 2011. *Twenty-First Century Ecosystems: Managing the Living World Two Centuries After Darwin: Report of a Symposium*. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press. [https://doi.org/10.17226/13109](https://doi.org/10.17226/13109).

That idea resonates strongly with how I think about biology. The molecular tools now available to us make it possible to study biological diversity at extraordinary resolution, but understanding that diversity also requires paying attention to organisms, their environments, and their evolutionary histories.

Outside my main research, I study the taxonomy and natural history of Southeast Asian blue-banded bees (*Amegilla*), in conversation and collaboration with wild-bee researchers in Thailand. I also enjoy photographing nature (especially flowers, bees, and birds) and maintain a longstanding interest in visual design and scientific communication.

## Art, biodesign & collaborative practice

Alongside scientific research and natural history, I have been involved in projects that bring biology into conversation with art, design, technology, and culture. These include collaborations with Henry Tan and the FREAK Lab community, the Molecular Soundtrack protein-design project, and an advisory role for Phantom Dining.
