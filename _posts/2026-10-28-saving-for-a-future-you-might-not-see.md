When you have some money, do you spend it or keep it? Part of the answer depends on two kinds of uncertainty: how stable your situation will be, and whether you will be there to experience it. In general, you tend to save more when the future is uncertain, while implicitly assuming that you will still be around when it arrives.

Sounds logical for a human. But what if you were a bacterium? Could you know anything about your future at all?

*E. coli* moving through a gut tends to meet a predictable sequence of sugars. When it senses lactose, it [starts preparing for maltose](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08112) before it arrives. Nobody taught an individual cell that order. Generations of natural selection wired the correlation into its gene regulation.<sup class="note-ref"><a href="#note-1" id="note-ref-1" aria-label="Note 1">1</a></sup>

But the present doesn’t always carry a clue to what comes next. Sometimes the only lesson the past holds is that conditions change.

Some bacteria do what you would do with money: keep some. A soil bacterium called *Cupriavidus necator*<sup class="note-ref"><a href="#note-2" id="note-ref-2" aria-label="Note 2">2</a></sup> stores part of the carbon it takes in as tiny granules of PHB, a polyester that can be processed into biodegradable plastic. The granules act as a temporary reserve. In an industrial fermenter, where carbon keeps arriving after nitrogen has run out, the granules can make up half or more of the cell’s dry weight. When conditions change, the cell can break PHB down and spend what it saved.

In what kind of situation is that reserve worth keeping? That is something we can actually test. In a [recent preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.08.717106v1), Jiaqi Huang, Adam Arkin and colleagues<sup class="note-ref"><a href="#note-3" id="note-ref-3" aria-label="Note 3">3</a></sup> followed thousands of individual *C. necator* cells as carbon and nitrogen came and went. When nutrients were plentiful, normal cells and a mutant unable to make PHB grew at essentially the same rate. The difference appeared when conditions changed. In one run tracking about 40,000 cells, after external carbon disappeared, normal lineages produced about 30% more offspring before they stopped dividing. And when nitrogen returned after starvation, the mutant waited about two hours before dividing again, while normal cells resumed almost immediately.

The cell does not need to know exactly what happens next for saving to pay. Its reserve only needs to stay useful across enough of the changes its lineage encounters.

That answers one kind of uncertainty. The other is still open. You save money assuming that eventually you will be there to spend it.

What happens when the saver isn’t?
